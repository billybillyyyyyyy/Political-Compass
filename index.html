A political compass test that measures what you actually think.

Most compass tests ask vague, loaded statements, count your answers with hidden weights, and drop nearly everyone in the same corner. This one asks plain questions about real trade-offs, keys them so that agreeing with everything cancels out, lets you skip what you don't know, and shows exactly how it scores you.

Choose a length

You can answer with a keyboard: 1 to 5 for strongly disagree to strongly agree, S to skip, M to mark a statement as important, Backspace to go back.

What you get

  • Your position on the classic compass (Economic and Authority), plus two axes the classic compass mashes together: Cultural (progressive vs traditional) and World (global vs national).
  • A plain-English reading of each axis and the three recognisable political positions closest to your answers, with the distance shown so you can see how close the match really is.
  • Your dot next to world leaders and politicians, with portraits. Search for anyone from Reagan to Thatcher to Mamdani, or browse the whole list.
  • A link you can share or save. Nothing is stored anywhere; the link contains your answers.

Neutral means you genuinely sit in the middle and counts toward the centre. Skip means you don't know or the statement doesn't apply; it is left out of the score entirely.

Closest recognisable positions

Rough placements of well-known positions, not measurements. Distance is on a 0 to 400 scale across all four axes; under 60 is a close match, over 120 means none of these really fits you.

Compare with public figures

Editorial placements of well-known people based on their public record, plotted next to you. Rough by nature. Hover a square on the map for the political label of that region.

Why this test is different

This was built after reading what people say is wrong with the popular tests: politicalcompass.org, 8values and its many clones (SapplyValues, 9Axes, PolitiScales), and the Pew typology quiz. The complaints repeat. Here is each one and what this test does about it.

The four axes

How it scores you

Each answer is a number: strongly disagree = -2, disagree = -1, neutral = 0, agree = +1, strongly agree = +2. Each statement has a direction (+1 or -1) saying which pole agreeing pushes toward. For each axis:

score = sum(direction x answer x weight) / sum(2 x weight) x 100

over the statements you answered on that axis. Weight is 1, or 1.5 if you flagged the statement as important. The result runs from -100 to +100. Skipped statements are left out of both the top and the bottom of the fraction. An axis with fewer than three answered statements is reported as not measured.

Because every axis has an equal number of statements keyed each way, someone who agrees with everything scores 0 on every axis instead of drifting. The results page also shows your average raw answer; if it is far from zero, you were agreeing or disagreeing with most statements regardless of direction, and you should treat the result with some care.

Where the public figures come from

The built-in list is editorial: each person was placed on the four axes by reading their record (votes, policies enacted, party platform, consistent public statements) and using the same scale as the test. Nobody on the list took the test. Treat the placements as landmarks, not measurements, and argue with them. The labels that appear when you hover a square of the map are broad families of positions for that region, drawn from how the terms are commonly used, not a claim about any one person.

What this test does not do

  • It does not tell you where you sit relative to the average voter in your country. Zero is "balanced answers", not "moderate". Calibrating against real populations needs survey data that no free online test has.
  • It does not measure everything. Foreign policy hawkishness, environmentalism and attitudes to technology are folded into the four axes or left out on purpose. Four axes is already at the limit of what most people can read at a glance; more axes look precise but mostly add noise.
  • It has not been validated on a large sample. The design follows what survey researchers recommend (balanced keying, item-specific wording, neutral and skip options, no valence items), but reliability figures would need real data. The full version will be the most stable if you retake it.
